Food-plant CIP: match each cleaning stage to the soil

MASEST Technical Team · July 26, 2026 · 2 min read

One circuit can hold two different soils

Protein, fat, yeast, and organic film are not the same problem as beer stone, carbonate scale, and rust. Asking one cleaner to do both jobs often stretches time, dose, and rinse water.

Start by mapping the circuit, soil, metallurgy, flow, temperature, dead legs, drain points, and current release endpoint. The cycle should follow the soil, not habit.

Use CR for the organic load

VertKleen CIP CR uses alkalinity, wetting, and sequestration to loosen protein, fat, yeast, krausen, and organic film. Flow and agitation carry released soil toward the drain.

The useful endpoint is not elapsed time alone. Track visible load, return clarity, concentration, temperature, and the rinse condition required by the facility procedure.

Use HCR for beer stone and scale

After the organic load and CR are rinsed out, VertKleen CIP HCR targets beer stone, carbonate scale, rust, and mineral film. It uses controlled mineral-removal chemistry instead of a blunt acid step.

Do not mix stages. Confirm chemical clearance before changing products, and match concentration and contact time to the deposit, circuit, and current product instructions.

Keep cleaning and sanitation distinct

Cleaning removes soil that can shield microorganisms or consume sanitizer. It does not replace the facility’s validated sanitation, verification, or production-release program.

The final rinse, sanitizer, inspection, testing, and release decision remain controlled by the food-safety plan, equipment requirements, and site SOP.

Measure return to production

A faster chemical reaction is useful only if the whole line returns sooner. Count preparation, circulation, rinsing, testing, corrective work, and release as completed-cycle cost.

Current labels, SDS files, dilution, and site controls still govern use. Lower hazard does not remove the need for disciplined chemical separation and verified release.
