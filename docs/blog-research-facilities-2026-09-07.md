# Facilities familiarity research — 2026-09-07

Purpose: give MASEST readers the feeling that the writer understands the shift, room, equipment, and purchasing problems in their facility. This is practical editorial research, not a claim-eligibility or product audit. Retrieved 2026-09-07.

The useful voice is operational: what is dirty, who has to touch it, what must be ready next, what gets inspected, and what a buyer can count. Keep cleaning, sanitizing, and disinfecting distinct. A cleaner can remove soil and residue; it does not replace a site’s approved sanitation or disinfection protocol.

## Primary source register

- **Schools and universities:** EPA, [Operations and Maintenance, Part of Indoor Air Quality Design Tools for Schools](https://www.epa.gov/iaq-schools/operations-and-maintenance-part-indoor-air-quality-design-tools-schools), retrieved 2026-09-07. EPA recommends a preventive maintenance plan with periodic checks, a work-order system that records work and cost for each piece of equipment, manuals, spare parts, and staff training.
- **Commercial property operations:** BOMA International, [CRE property management assessment](https://boma.org/resource/cre-property-management-assessment-your-building-operations-scorecard/), retrieved 2026-09-07. Its operations scorecard treats work-order management, preventive maintenance, building inspections, tenant experience, and communication as linked operating disciplines.
- **Commercial kitchens:** FDA, [2022 Food Code print version](https://www.fda.gov/media/184685/download), retrieved 2026-09-07. Food-contact surfaces must be properly cleaned to remove organic material before the required sanitizing step; a sanitizer wipe alone is not the full cleaning-and-sanitizing procedure. The Food Code also explains that the required sanitizer concentration and contact time follow the Code and the applicable EPA-registered label.
- **Gyms and community facilities:** CDC, [When and How to Clean and Disinfect a Facility](https://www.cdc.gov/hygiene/about/when-and-how-to-clean-and-disinfect-a-facility.html), retrieved 2026-09-07. The community-facility guidance explicitly includes gyms: clean high-touch surfaces regularly, clean other surfaces when visibly dirty, and disinfect when the situation and facility protocol call for it. It says to clean before sanitizing or disinfecting.
- **Drains and grease:** EPA, [Optimizing Operation, Maintenance, and Rehabilitation of Sanitary Sewer Collection Systems](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-10/documents/sso_optimizing_enitre_doc.pdf), retrieved 2026-09-07. EPA identifies fats, oils, and grease from restaurants and institutional food service—including hotels, nursing homes, hospitals, and schools—as major contributors to blockages and overflows. EPA’s [Sewer Cleaning and Inspection fact sheet](https://nepis.epa.gov/Exe/ZyPURL.cgi?Dockey=P10053G5.TXT), retrieved the same day, says interceptor cleaning frequency can range from twice a month to once every six months depending on grease discharge.
- **Healthcare environmental services:** CDC, [Environmental Cleaning Procedures](https://www.cdc.gov/healthcare-associated-infections/hcp/cleaning-global/procedures.html), retrieved 2026-09-07. Cleaning frequency and method should follow contamination probability, patient vulnerability, and exposure; schedules should name the responsible person, frequency, method, and detailed procedure.
- **General commercial O&M:** U.S. EPA ENERGY STAR, [Operation and Maintenance Best Practices](https://www.energystar.gov/buildings/save-energy-commercial-buildings/ways-save/om-best-practices), retrieved 2026-09-07. It connects proactive maintenance with fewer equipment malfunctions and occupant complaints, and recommends documenting operating conditions and tracking performance over time.

## Short editorial inserts by operating cluster

These are the companion JSON inserts in [`artifacts/blog-refresh/research-inserts-facilities.json`](../artifacts/blog-refresh/research-inserts-facilities.json). Each one is written for a precise existing paragraph in the current 35-post source and avoids turning an external source into a VertKleen endorsement.

### Schools and universities

Target: `school-university-facility-cleaning-plan`. Make the bell schedule, short shutdown, work order, air-handler, kitchen, gym, and return-to-use handoff feel familiar. Useful KPIs are minutes from isolation to reopening, repeat passes, rooms held out of service, and work orders closed with a documented finish.

### Hotels and property operations

Target: `hotel-property-turnover-facility-cleaning`; connect housekeeping, engineering, kitchens, laundry, pools, entrances, drains, guest access, and the next check-in. Useful KPIs are room or area release time, repeat calls, guest-facing defects, work orders by asset, and product consumed per completed turnover.

### Commercial kitchens

Target: `commercial-kitchen-degreasing-guide`; describe the closing shift, cool-down, hood/filter and cookline soil, rinse, inspection, and required sanitation release. Use “clean first, then sanitize when the food-safety program requires it,” never “degreaser disinfects.” Useful KPIs are minutes until release, repeat passes, rinse/recovery water, and cost per completed close.

### Gyms and fitness facilities

Target: `commercial-gym-cleaning-checklist`; describe the opening/closing route across cardio consoles, benches, mats, locker rooms, showers, tile, and drains. Keep equipment manufacturer instructions and any separate disinfection step in the workflow. Useful KPIs are equipment groups completed, wet-zone rework, missed high-touch points, and time until members can use the area.

### Drains and condensate lines

Targets: `commercial-drain-odor-control-guide` and `hvac-condensate-drain-line-cleaning-guide`. Start with the fault—dry trap, organic film, partial blockage, bad slope, pan backup, or unknown branch—then define the finish as restored flow, no recurring odor, a dry pan, or a known escalation. Useful KPIs are days to odor return, repeat calls, drains cleared per labor hour, and units passing a full-flow check.

## Editorial guardrails

- Use recognizable jobs and finish standards: “the next class,” “the next check-in,” “the next member,” “the next meal service,” “the pan drains,” or “the room is ready.”
- Name the part that causes the call: cookline, hood filter, condensate pan, trap, strainer, heat exchanger, cardio console, locker-room tile, pool edge, or resident-care equipment.
- Treat product choice as one step inside the site’s work plan. Preserve lockout, access, PPE, runoff/recovery, food-safety, infection-prevention, and manufacturer controls.
- Do not say a degreaser, odor-control product, or general cleaner replaces sanitizing or disinfecting. For kitchens, gyms, healthcare, and senior living, retain the facility’s separate approved protocol.
